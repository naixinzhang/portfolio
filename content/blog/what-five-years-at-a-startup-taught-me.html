TL;DR

In one line: Five years as a data scientist at a startup taught me that impact beats methodology, direction beats effort, and the only things you fully control — your health, your learning, your savings rate — compound quietly while everything else churns.

You should care if: you're early in a data career and optimizing for the wrong scoreboard.

Do this today: ask of your current project: "if this succeeds perfectly, what business decision changes?" If you can't answer, that's the finding.

I joined a startup five years ago as a data scientist. The company changed shape under me several times — new products, new orgs, new definitions of what "data science" even meant there. Most of what I believed on day one turned out to be wrong, or at least incomplete. This is the list I wish someone had handed me at the start. It comes in three parts: the work itself, the career around the work, and the life around the career.

On doing the work

1. Business impact is the only scoreboard

Titles are building blocks, not identities. Data scientist, analyst, engineer, PM — at a startup these are just different tools pointed at the same goal, and the goal doesn't care which tool you are. The people who grew fastest around me were the ones who stopped asking "is this my job?" and started asking "does this move the thing we're all here to move?" Every project I'm proud of, I can name the decision it changed or the number it moved. Every project I regret, I can only name the method it used.

2. Don't chase fancy methodology — ship fast

The elegant model that ships next quarter loses to the crude heuristic that ships Friday. Not because rigor doesn't matter — my whole blog is an argument that it does — but because at a startup the biggest source of error isn't your estimator, it's building the wrong thing entirely. Shipping fast is how you find that out while it's still cheap. The fancy version is something you earn the right to build after the crude version proves the problem is real.

3. Allow yourself to fail

Almost everything durable I know, I learned from something that went wrong. The projects that worked on the first try taught me very little — I couldn't tell you which of my choices mattered, because none of them got stress-tested. Failure is the only experiment with a proper control group. The skill isn't avoiding failure; it's failing on small, recoverable bets and actually doing the post-mortem instead of quietly moving on.

4. Be proactive, be opinionated, say it out loud

Nobody hands you the interesting problems. The quiet, competent person who waits to be asked gets staffed on whatever's left over. Having an opinion — a falsifiable, "here's what I think we should do and why" opinion — is what turns you from a service function into a thought partner. You'll be wrong sometimes, in public. That's fine; see lesson 3. Being wrong out loud and correcting course builds more trust than being silently right ever did.

5. Your analysis doesn't exist until it's communicated

Having an opinion is half the skill; the other half is translation. Some of the best analytical work I've seen died in a slide deck because it was presented as analysis instead of as a decision — methodology first, punchline on slide nine, in vocabulary the room didn't share. The number that moves a business is the one the decision-maker can repeat to their boss without you there. I now spend as much time on the narrative as on the numbers, and I've stopped feeling guilty about it: the narrative is not packaging around the work, it is the last mile of the work. Writing publicly is the compounding version of the same skill — every post is a rep.

6. Take ownership — everything is learnable

The most useful belief I adopted was that there is no skill on the other side of a wall. Infrastructure, front-end, sales calls, legal review — it's all learnable to the depth the problem requires. Once you believe that, "that's not my area" stops being a reason and starts being an excuse. Ownership means the outcome is yours, including the parts that require skills you don't have yet.

7. Set goals and milestones — for yourself, not just your projects

Startups are ambient chaos. If you don't define what a successful quarter looks like for you, the chaos will define it for you, and you'll look up after a year of being busy with nothing to show that compounds. I treat my own growth like a project: explicit goals, explicit milestones, explicit reviews. It sounds bureaucratic. It's the opposite — it's the only defense against drift.

8. Work smart, not just hard — direction dominates effort

If the direction is wrong, all the hard work is meaningless. This is the painful multiplication: impact = direction × effort, and direction can be zero or negative. The weeks I spent perfecting something nobody needed were not neutral — they cost the weeks I could have spent on the thing that mattered. Before going heads-down, the highest-leverage hour is the one spent making sure heads-down is pointed the right way.

On building a career

9. Senior roles come from connections, not cold applications

This one I resisted the longest, because it feels unfair. But the pattern is unambiguous: past a certain level, roles are filled by "who do we know who can do this?" long before a job posting exists. Building connections isn't schmoozing — it's doing visible work, writing publicly, staying current on where the field is moving, and being the person who comes to mind. The random-apply pipeline is where you go when the network pipeline is empty, and by then you're competing with everyone.

10. Reputation compounds like everything else

The mechanism behind the previous lesson isn't visibility — it's trust. Being the person whose numbers nobody re-checks, whose "this is ready" means it's ready, whose estimate of two weeks means two weeks. That reputation is built in small deposits over years and can be spent down in one overclaimed result, so I protect it the way I'd protect any compounding asset: I'd rather report a smaller effect honestly than a bigger one I can't fully defend. People don't refer the person they've met; they refer the person they trust.

11. Past a certain point, your impact is what you enable

Everything above is an individual-contributor lesson, and for the first few years that's the right frame. But somewhere along the way the math changes: the ceiling on what one person can ship is low, and the ceiling on what a person who makes five other people better can ship is not. Mentoring a junior, writing the doc that becomes how the team does things, asking the question in review that saves someone a month — none of it shows up in your own project list, and all of it eventually outweighs your own project list. The shift is uncomfortable because the feedback loop is slower and the credit is diffuse. It's also the actual job, one level up.

12. Keep learning, permanently

The half-life of a data science skillset keeps shrinking. What I was hired for five years ago is partially automated now; what I do now barely existed then. The only stable strategy is to treat learning as a standing line item — not something you do between jobs, but something the job is never allowed to fully crowd out.

13. AI evolves — statistics is the moat

Here's the asymmetry hidden inside "keep learning": not everything you learn decays at the same rate. The tools I obsessed over three years ago are museum pieces; the frameworks churn every six months. But the statistical foundations — sampling, uncertainty, experimental design, knowing when a difference is real — have only become more valuable as AI made everything else easier. When the tooling layer is commoditized for everyone, the moat is knowing whether the number the tool produced means anything. That foundation isn't a degree you earned once; it rusts. I go back and brush it deliberately, the way you'd maintain anything you can't afford to lose.

On everything else

14. Keep a regular workout — it's one of the few things you control

Roadmaps get cut, reorgs happen, markets turn — none of it is up to you. Whether you trained this week is entirely up to you. That's worth more than the fitness itself: in a job made of uncertainty, having one domain where input reliably produces output keeps you sane. It's also the cheapest performance enhancer available for a thinking job.

15. Start investing early — buy experiences, not luxury goods

Money compounds, and the variable that matters most is time in, not timing. Starting early with boring index investments beats starting late with clever ones. And on the spending side: the experiences I bought — travel, races, time with people — appreciated in memory; the objects depreciated in a drawer. Five years on, that asymmetry is obvious in a way it wasn't at the first paycheck.

16. There is no work-life balance at the frontier — and that's a choice

I'll say the unfashionable part honestly: if you genuinely want to achieve something outsized, there is no comfortable equilibrium. It takes a level of dedication that "balance" doesn't describe. The startup years were not balanced, and pretending otherwise would be revisionist. What I'd add now: this is a choice with a price, not a law of nature — you should make it deliberately, for a bounded season, with your eyes open. Lessons 14 and 15 are what make the intensity survivable. Lesson 8 is what makes it worth anything at all.

The compressed version

Move the business, not the methodology. Ship, fail, own it, say what you think — and make sure it lands. Point the effort before you spend it, then multiply it through other people. Let your network, your reputation, your knowledge, your health, and your money all compound — they run on the same math. Chase the frontier, but keep the statistical foundations polished — that's the moat. And if you choose intensity, choose it on purpose.

Five years in, that's the whole list. Ask me again in five more.
